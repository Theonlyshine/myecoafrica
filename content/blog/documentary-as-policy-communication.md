Germany didn't just build the Energiewende. It narrated it.

For two decades, German public broadcasting, documentary filmmakers, and institutional communications teams produced a consistent body of media that made the energy transition legible to ordinary Germans — what it cost, why it mattered, what the tradeoffs were. By the time solar panels were visible on rooftops in every town, the story had been told so many times and through so many formats that the infrastructure felt inevitable rather than imposed. The media did not come after the transition. It was part of how the transition happened.

That is not a European accident. It is a communications strategy. And it has an application for every country on the African continent that is currently building things nobody knows about.

---

**Denmark and the Story of Wind**

Denmark's wind energy leadership is globally recognised. Denmark supplies more than half its electricity from wind. Danish wind technology companies — Vestas, Ørsted — are dominant globally. Denmark is, in the global imagination, the country that figured out wind.

That reputation was not an automatic consequence of building wind turbines. It was constructed, deliberately, through decades of sustained documentary production, institutional communications, and media investment that made the story of Danish wind a coherent global narrative.

The Danes built the turbines. They also built the story. And the story attracted talent, investment, and policy partnerships from around the world at a scale that the turbines alone would not have produced. The media was not the decoration on top of the infrastructure strategy. It was part of the competitive advantage.

**What Africa Is Building (That Nobody Knows About)**

Morocco's Noor solar complex is one of the largest concentrated solar power installations in the world. At full capacity, it powers over a million homes and positions Morocco as a renewable energy exporter to Europe. It is an infrastructure achievement of genuine global significance.

Most people outside North Africa have never heard of it.

Kenya's Lake Turkana Wind Farm — 365 turbines, the largest wind farm on the African continent — added 310 megawatts to Kenya's grid when it came online. It was built by a consortium of African and international investors, developed over more than a decade, and represents exactly the kind of long-term infrastructure commitment that Kenya's energy success story is built on.

Most people outside East Africa have never heard of it.

Senegal's solar village programme has been quietly electrifying rural communities for years. Nigeria has solar microgrids operating in rural communities across the northeast and northwest. Ghana has wind and solar installations. South Africa is building one of the largest hydrogen production facilities on the continent.

These things exist. They are real. They are being built by Africans and by institutions investing in Africa. And the absence of a sustained, high-quality media record of that building is not a minor communications gap. It is a strategic failure that affects how capital flows, how policy is made, and how the continent is positioned in every global conversation about the energy transition.

**Why One Documentary Is Not Enough**

This is the point that is most frequently misunderstood by organisations that commission sustainability media: a documentary made in 2020 does not do the same work in 2026 that a consistent media presence does.

Institutional memory is built through repetition. A funder who saw one film about solar adoption in rural Ghana three years ago does not carry that into the next investment decision. A policymaker who read one report about West Africa's energy landscape in 2021 is not drawing on it today. The world moves fast and the attention of the people who make decisions about African infrastructure is spread across dozens of competing priorities.

What breaks through is not the exceptional single piece of work. It is the cumulative body of work — the consistent presence, the sustained narrative, the media record that grows over time — that becomes the reference point for anyone trying to understand what is happening in a sector.

Germany's energy transition narrative was not built by one documentary. It was built by hundreds of documentaries, thousands of news features, dozens of institutional communications campaigns, over twenty years. Denmark's wind story was not built in a year.

Africa is behind on this. Not because the stories don't exist — they do. Because the institutional commitment to producing and distributing them consistently has not been there.

**What This Means for Institutions Building in Africa**

If you are a development finance institution, an energy company, a government body, or an NGO building something in Africa's sustainability transition, the absence of a media strategy is not neutral. It means:

The capital that should follow your work doesn't know the work exists. The policy alignment that your project needs is harder to achieve because the broader story you are part of hasn't been told. The talent — the engineers, the analysts, the programme managers — who could join what you are building don't see themselves in the story of it because there is no story.

A media strategy for a sustainability institution is not a marketing expense. It is infrastructure. It builds the institutional credibility, the policy relationships, and the talent pipeline that every long-term project requires.

The organisations in Africa's sustainability space that will be most influential in a decade are not necessarily the ones with the most funding today. They are the ones that build a consistent, honest, high-quality media record of what they are doing and why.

Between 2000 and 2024, Nigeria spent an estimated [$20 billion on electricity infrastructure](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2023/08/nigeria-has-spent-about-20bn-on-power-sector-in-23-years-with-little-to-show-expert/). As of 2024, the average Nigerian still experiences between [10 and 20 hours of power outage per day](https://nerc.gov.ng/index.php/library/documents/func-startdown/1398/). That math does not add up. And it is not supposed to — because Africa's electricity crisis is not primarily a story about technical capacity. It is a story about where money goes, who makes decisions about it, and what happens when no one is accountable across administrations.

The technical problem is solvable. The governance problem is what keeps it from being solved.

---

**Nigeria: The Reform That Never Arrived**

Nigeria's power sector has been "reforming" for more than two decades. It is worth tracking what that has actually looked like.

Under Olusegun Obasanjo (1999–2007), the [National Electric Power Authority became the Power Holding Company of Nigeria](https://www.premiumtimesng.com/news/top-news/88213-why-nigerias-power-sector-keeps-failing.html). Generation capacity expanded on paper. The distribution infrastructure — the poles, the transformers, the last-mile connections that actually get power to homes and businesses — did not keep pace. The reform was structural; the delivery was not.

Under Umaru Yar'adua and Goodluck Jonathan (2007–2015), the government committed [$16 billion to the power sector through the Presidential Action Committee on Power](https://www.premiumtimesng.com/news/headlines/437088-nigeria-spent-16-billion-on-power-sector-without-improving-supply-reps.html). Actual electricity output at the end of that period was not significantly higher than at the beginning. Privatisation of the distribution companies (DISCOs) in 2013 was supposed to inject private efficiency into the system. Instead, most of the DISCOs were acquired by companies that lacked either the capital or the capacity to run them. The assets changed hands. The service did not improve.

Under Muhammadu Buhari (2015–2023), a "national emergency" in power was declared within the first year of administration. The administration ended with roughly the same installed generation capacity and significantly worse performance on the system overall, partly due to gas supply failures, partly due to transmission constraints, and partly due to the same procurement and accountability problems that preceded it.

The pattern across all three periods is the same: each administration inherited the problem, announced a programme, spent funds, and left the problem for the next person.

**Ghana: When a Crisis Gets a Name**

Between 2012 and 2016, Ghana experienced power cuts so regular and so long — 12 to 18 hours per day in many areas — that they acquired a name: [Dumsor](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-africa-32928753), meaning "off-on" in Twi. It became a defining feature of daily life for Ghanaian businesses and households for nearly four years.

The government's primary response was to accelerate the procurement of emergency diesel power plants — a short-term fix that became permanent infrastructure for an entire generation of businesses. Those businesses built their cost models around running generators. When grid supply eventually improved, the transition back to grid dependence was slow because the trust had been broken.

As of 2024, Ghana's generation capacity is significantly better than it was in the Dumsor era. But the financial architecture of the electricity sector remains broken. The [Electricity Company of Ghana (ECG) operates at a structural loss](https://www.graphic.com.gh/news/general-news/ecg-makes-losses-on-every-unit-of-electricity-sold.html) because tariffs are set below cost recovery for political reasons. A technically improved grid running on a financially unsustainable model is a future crisis, not a solved problem.

**Senegal: The Fork in the Road**

Senegal's offshore gas discoveries beginning in 2014 — culminating in the [SNE field development and the GTA LNG project](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/senegal-brings-first-oil-online-inaugurates-sangomar-field-2024-06-11/) — have put the country at a decision point that every resource-rich African country eventually faces.

The revenue from gas extraction can fund the renewable infrastructure that would give Senegal energy independence within a generation. Or it can fund consumption, procurement corruption, and short-term political spending, leaving the country dependent on fossil infrastructure precisely when the global market is transitioning away from it.

As of 2026, the indicators are mixed. Senegal has continued to invest in solar and wind capacity. The [Taiba N'Diaye wind farm](https://www.taibandiayes.com/), completed in 2021, is a genuinely significant infrastructure achievement. But the institutional governance of the petroleum revenue remains underdeveloped, and the precedent from other African resource discoveries is not encouraging.

**South Africa: The Most Complete Case Study**

Eskom is what institutional decay looks like when it reaches the physical infrastructure layer.

In 2000, [Eskom was considered one of the best-run utilities in the world](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2023-01-30-how-eskom-went-from-one-of-the-worlds-best-utilities-to-the-worst/). By 2023, South Africa was experiencing Stage 8 load-shedding — the most severe category — with up to 12 to 14 hours of daily outages affecting the entire country. That deterioration did not happen because South Africa's engineers forgot how to maintain power plants. It happened because [procurement corruption over approximately 15 years](https://www.businesslive.co.za/bd/national/2023-02-15-state-capture-broke-eskom/) resulted in deferred maintenance, inflated contracts, and the gutting of the institutional capacity that had made the utility functional.

[Stage 8 load-shedding cost the South African economy an estimated R500 million per hour](https://businesstech.co.za/news/energy/644927/this-is-how-much-load-shedding-is-costing-south-africa-every-hour/). That is the financial expression of institutional failure at scale. It is the number that answers the question: why does governance quality in the energy sector matter?

**Kenya: What Continuity Looks Like**

Kenya is a useful counter-case, because it is one of the few countries in sub-Saharan Africa that has shown meaningful improvement in electricity access over the same 20-year period.

The difference is not technology. It is policy continuity. Kenya's investment in geothermal energy at Olkaria — [now one of the largest geothermal plants in Africa](https://www.kengen.co.ke/index.php/our-business/power-plants/geothermal.html) — was not a single administration's project. It was a 20-year commitment that survived multiple government changes, multiple budget cycles, and multiple periods of political uncertainty. By 2024, [Kenya's electricity mix is among the most renewable on the continent](https://www.irena.org/Energy-Transition/Country-profiles/Kenya), with over 90 percent of generation coming from clean sources.

The lesson from Kenya is not that geothermal is the answer for every country. It is that infrastructure at scale requires policy commitments that outlast the politicians who make them. Kenya created institutional structures — through KenGen, through long-term procurement agreements — that made the investment survivable across administrations. That is what Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa have consistently failed to build.

**The Pattern Across Five Countries**

The five cases above are not isolated. They share the same failure architecture:

Short political cycles misaligned with long infrastructure timelines. A power plant takes 5 to 10 years to plan, finance, and build. An African head of state has 4 to 5 years before the next election. The incentive to announce rather than deliver is structural.

Procurement systems that prioritise political connection over technical competence. This is the most direct mechanism of failure. When contracts are awarded to companies with the right connections rather than the right capacity, the infrastructure either doesn't get built or gets built badly.

No independent accountability across administrations. The officials who make the decisions are rarely still in office — or in the country — when the consequences arrive. The absence of institutional memory and cross-administration accountability means the same mistakes recur with minimal friction.

**The Energy Gap Is Not Background Noise**

Every hour of load-shedding in South Africa costs approximately [R500 million](https://businesstech.co.za/news/energy/644927/this-is-how-much-load-shedding-is-costing-south-africa-every-hour/). In Nigeria, the energy crisis is not background noise — it is an active selection mechanism. The businesses that survive and grow are the ones that can afford generators, inverters, and the ongoing fuel cost. That means the energy crisis is also a competition policy: it structurally advantages well-capitalised businesses and systematically disadvantages small operators. The person selling from a market stall cannot absorb ₦40,000 per month in fuel costs. The supermarket chain can. The playing field is not level, and the energy failure is part of why.

---

**What Changes This**

The technical answer — more generation capacity, better grids, renewable investment — is known. It is not complicated. What is complicated is building the institutional architecture that allows the technical solution to be implemented and maintained across political cycles.

That requires independent regulatory bodies with genuine enforcement authority, long-term procurement frameworks that survive administration changes, transparent revenue management, and a media and civil society environment that can hold governments accountable across the full 10 to 20 year cycle of infrastructure delivery.

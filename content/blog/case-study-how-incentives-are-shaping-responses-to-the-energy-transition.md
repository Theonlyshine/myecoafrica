I find it very interesting that your environment largely defines your understanding of global systems.

A good example would be this comment made by a Nigerian observer.

As a Nigerian myself, I find his logic completely understandable. For a region where solar is a symbol for wealth, comfort, luxury, and an escape from a grid that is NEVER reliable, it’s is not far fetched that a Nigerian considers it an unnecessary or bizarre purchase for people with stable power supply.

However, the more we zoom out, the clearer the puzzle pieces: Our environment does not just **dictate how we process new/existing technology (or information), the systems in place can also shape our response to it.** 

**And for the sake of this essay, we’ll use this comment as a Case Study.**

First, I want you to think deeply about this: Why should two people in different parts of the world look at the same solar panel and read two completely different meanings into them?

I can only think of one reason: SYSTEM DESIGN.

In a well planned system design, a consumer does not buy solar to solve a power outage. They buy it to **control personal expenses and generate financial returns**.

Through schemes like the Smart Export Guarantee (SEG), homeowners sell excess electricity back to the grid. The house becomes a mini power plant that generates passive income. Also, in the long run, the system eventually pays for itself in 6 to 8 years. Everything after that is pure profit, while simultaneously increasing the market resale value of the home.

In this system, solar is unequivocally an **Asse**t, a tool that puts money back into the owner’s pocket. It is almost irrational to not have a unit installed in your home.

However, in Nigeria and many other parts of Africa, Solar is not bought as an investment; it is bought out of **necessity for basic survival**.

To add to that, the Batteries are consumable hardware and replacing an aging battery bank every 4 to 6 years (if you’re lucky to get the best of them) adds up to a recurring, multi-million Naira capital outlay.

Also, without functional residential net metering, you cannot sell your excess power back to the grid. The energy your panels produce beyond your immediate battery capacity is simply…LOST.

**Why is this case-study important?**

Because it is easy to accuse this observer or people in developing markets of having a "limited perspective" for failing to appreciate the global energy transition. 

But mental models do not exist in a vacuum; they are shaped by the incentive structures around us.

When a system forces you to spend millions of Naira just to keep your fridge running, laptops charged, phones turned on, fans rolling, etc. You naturally perceive that technology as an unavoidable tax on living but if that system allowed you to trade your excess power for cash? You’ll naturally perceive that exact same technology as a wealth-building tool or something that JUST MAKES SENSE.

If we want emerging markets to embrace renewables at scale, we must move beyond preaching about "sustainability" and start redesigning the underlying market structures.

Until we put structures in place to establish functional net metering in Africa, low-cost consumer finance, solar will remain a luxury expense for survival while the rest of the world leverages it as an engine for economic growth.

**T**he Nigerian observer may be partly ignorant, but that ignorance is a response to a broken incentive structure. To change how people view technology, you don't just change their minds, you change the system that governs their daily realities.

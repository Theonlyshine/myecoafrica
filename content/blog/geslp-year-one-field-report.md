The Global Energy and Sustainability Leadership Program launched its first full cohort year in May 2026. This report documents what we learned — from schools, from students, from the administrative staff who had to supervise a new curriculum, and from the data we collected on learning outcomes and sustainability literacy across the cohort.

The core question GESLP was designed to answer is whether sustainability education at secondary level is structurally achievable in contexts where school resources are constrained, teacher bandwidth is limited, and curricular flexibility is narrow. The year one data gives us a provisional answer: it is achievable if the design assumption is zero additional burden on school staff.

This report covers deployment across 7 states in Nigeria, documents the operational model that allowed free delivery at scale, and presents a preliminary analysis of student outcome data from pre- and post-cohort assessments.

It is written for educational institutions considering adoption, development finance partners assessing impact metrics, and corporate or governmental entities evaluating sponsorship or co-deployment at scale.

---

**What We Set Out to Test**

The design premise of GESLP was not that sustainability education is good in the abstract. The premise was more specific: that a secondary school in a constrained context — limited budget, stretched teachers, narrow curriculum window — could integrate a rigorous sustainability leadership programme without it becoming a burden that collapses after the first term.

Most educational programmes that arrive from outside the school system fail on this axis. They require the school to do work the school was not resourced to do. GESLP was built on the opposite assumption: the programme absorbs the complexity so the school does not have to.

Year one was the first real test of that assumption at scale.

---

**Deployment: 7 States, Zero Cost to Schools**

In the first cohort year, GESLP deployed across 7 states in Nigeria. Every school that participated received the full programme at zero cost: curriculum materials, facilitator training, assessment tools, and coordination support. The cost of delivery was absorbed entirely by MyEcoAfrica and its partners.

The operational model that made this possible relied on a small number of design decisions that reduced the per-school cost significantly: a facilitator model that trains local educators rather than deploying external staff, a curriculum that integrates into existing timetable structures rather than requiring additional time, and assessment tools that generate data without requiring specialist administration.

These are not features that emerged from iteration after launch. They were design requirements from the beginning, because the programme was built for contexts where the school has no capacity to absorb additional cost or complexity.

---

**Student Outcomes: Preliminary Data**

Pre- and post-cohort assessments were administered across the full cohort to measure change in sustainability literacy — the working definition being a student's ability to identify sustainability challenges in their local context, name institutional actors responsible for addressing them, and propose a structured response.

The preliminary data shows meaningful improvement on all three dimensions across the cohort. The most significant gains were in the third dimension — structured response — which is also the dimension most directly targeted by the programme's project-based learning component.

The least consistent results were in the institutional actors dimension, which varied significantly by state. This variation tracks predictably with differences in civic education infrastructure across the states, and suggests a refinement target for year two: strengthening the institutional literacy component in contexts where baseline civic knowledge is lower.

Full outcome data, disaggregated by state and school type, will be published in the final year one report in Q3 2026.

---

**What Schools Said**

The feedback from school administrators and supervising teachers was consistent on one point: the zero-burden design worked. Schools that had initial reservations about adding a new programme to an already stretched timetable reported that the integration was manageable and that the programme did not displace existing curriculum priorities.

The feedback that matters most for year two planning came from students. Across the cohort, the most frequently cited outcome was not knowledge gain but orientation shift: students reported a change in how they understood the relationship between local problems and systemic causes. That shift — from "this is just how things are" to "these conditions are produced by decisions, and decisions can be changed" — is the foundational literacy that everything else in the programme is designed to build toward.

In Ibadan, a child lacks light because the grid has been out for over 17 hours in a day. The same thing happens across Ghana. The same thing happens across Nairobi. These things have become the median experience across West and East Africa, and they are connected.

Africa's energy, health, and education sectors each have their own history. They have their own policy frameworks and their own international development programmes. But they share an architecture of failure that is worth naming clearly: institutions built to administer, not to deliver.

**Energy**

Nigeria has approximately [85 million people without reliable electricity access](https://www.iea.org/reports/africa-energy-outlook-2022), making it the country with the largest energy access deficit in the world. The generation capacity on paper has grown. However, the transmission and distribution infrastructure has not kept pace. Funds have been allocated across multiple administrations. Sector reforms have been announced. Privatisation was supposed to fix what government could not.

As of today, the average Nigerian business owner spends between [30,000 and 100,000 naira per month](https://www.nbs.gov.ng) on diesel to keep their business running. That money is not an investment. It is not savings. It is not growth. It is a tax on institutional failure, paid directly by the people least able to afford it.

This is not unique to Nigeria. Ghana's Dumsor crisis between [2012 and 2016](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-africa-32928753) saw 12 to 18 hour daily power outages so normalised they earned a name. The fix was diesel generators. A short-term emergency response became permanent infrastructure for an entire generation of businesses.

**Health**

[Sub-Saharan Africa carries approximately 25 percent of the global disease burden while operating with roughly 3 percent of the world's health workers.](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240074323) Nigeria spends an estimated [$1 billion annually on medical tourism](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2022/07/nigeria-loses-1bn-annually-to-medical-tourism-experts/), its own citizens travelling to India and the United Kingdom to access care that should be available domestically. That figure is not a healthcare statistic. It is a measurement of institutional failure, expressed in foreign exchange.

Rural clinics across the continent operate without basic diagnostic equipment. Maternal mortality rates in sub-Saharan Africa remain among the highest in the world despite decades of international health programmes. The funding comes in. The outcomes do not follow.

**Education**

[UNESCO projects that Africa will need 15 million additional teachers by 2030](https://uis.unesco.org/en/topic/teachers) to meet enrolment demand. But the teacher shortage, serious as it is, is not the deepest problem. The deeper problem is that the curriculum in most African secondary schools was not designed for the economy that is actually emerging. It was designed for an administrative class in a colonial economy. We have inherited the structure and mostly kept it.

A student passing through that curriculum is not being prepared to build, to analyse, to create, or to lead in a 21st-century African context. They are being processed through a system optimised for compliance, not capacity.

**Why These Failures Connect**

An energy failure is not just inconvenient. When a hospital generator runs out of diesel mid-shift, the energy failure becomes a health failure. When a student cannot study after dark because there is no power and no money for fuel, the energy failure becomes an education failure.

These sectors are not independent. They form a system. The question that matters for institutions, for policymakers, and for every citizen who has been told that reform is coming is not whether the problem is real. The question is why the same failure keeps reproducing across different administrations, different sectors, and different countries, and what it would actually take to break the pattern.
